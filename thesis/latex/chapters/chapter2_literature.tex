\section{Foundations of Software Quality and Architecture}
\label{sec:foundations}

Any evaluation of coding agents against structural quality criteria needs to be grounded in what
those criteria actually are and where they come from. This section covers three layers: design
principles, code smells, and architectural metrics. They are not independent, they reinforce
each other in ways that matter for the analysis in later chapters.

\subsection{Design Principles}
\label{sec:design-principles}

Robert C. Martin's SOLID principles \citep{martin2000solid} remain the most widely cited normative
framework for object-oriented design (Figure~\ref{fig:solid-wheel}). The Single Responsibility
Principle (SRP) holds that a class should have one reason to change; the Open-Closed Principle
(OCP) favours extending behaviour over modifying existing code; the Liskov Substitution Principle
(LSP) requires that subclasses can substitute for their parents without breaking callers; the
Interface Segregation Principle (ISP) argues against broad interfaces that force clients to depend
on methods they do not use; and the Dependency Inversion Principle (DIP) decouples high-level
modules from low-level ones through abstraction. These are heuristics, not laws, but they connect
to a long metrics literature, cohesion, coupling, and inheritance depth are all measurable
proxies for the qualities SOLID targets, which is what makes automated assessment possible at all.
Larman's GRASP patterns \citep{larman2004grasp} complement SOLID at a finer grain, giving
assignment-level guidance, Information Expert, Low Coupling, High Cohesion, for where
responsibility should sit.

Evidence on how well agents follow these principles is recent and uneven. The NITR benchmark
\citep{zhu2026nitr} evaluates whether agent edits preserve maintainable structure, not just
behaviour, and reports that agents handle responsibility-decomposition tasks far better than
dependency-control tasks, DIP, the principle needing the most deliberate abstraction, is the
hardest. It also finds cases that pass functional tests while failing structural checks, which is
the more consequential point: test suites, as usually constructed, do not see these problems. A
separate study prompting LLMs to detect SOLID violations directly \citep{pehlivan2025solid} reaches
the same conclusion from the other side, detection accuracy is high for structurally-obvious
principles but degrades on DIP and with rising code complexity. This thesis's own results
(Chapter~\ref{ch:results}) independently corroborate that DIP is the least tractable of the five.

A note on the status of this evidence. The agent-evaluation literature is moving faster than peer
review, and a large share of the work reviewed in this chapter, including most of the 2026 studies
compared in Table~\ref{tab:comparative-synthesis}, exists only as preprints. They are cited here
because they are the closest available work and because ignoring them would misrepresent the state
of the field, but they have not been refereed, and where this thesis leans on one it says which
claim it is leaning on rather than treating the source as settled.

\subsection{Code Smells}
\label{sec:code-smells}

The term ``code smell'' was popularised by \citet{fowler1999refactoring}, which catalogued
patterns, Long Method, God Class, Data Clumps, Feature Envy, Primitive Obsession, and others,
that signal design problems without being outright bugs. The taxonomy's value is a shared
vocabulary for what is going wrong and where. The empirical picture linking smells to defects is
real but mixed rather than uniform: several large studies associate smelly classes with higher
change- and fault-proneness, while others find some smells (including God Class) less fault-prone
once class size is controlled for, a caution this thesis inherits, since its own God-Class proxy
is deliberately size-plus-cohesion rather than size alone (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}). Automated
detection tools, SonarQube, PMD, CodeScene, the CK metrics suite, operationalise the taxonomy
through threshold-based rules, which is what makes cross-codebase comparison possible.

In AI-generated code, the smell picture is qualitatively different from human-generated equivalents
in ways that go beyond a simple density increase. \citet{zhu2026smells} describe what they call a
\emph{Volume-Quality Inverse Law}: code volume is a near-perfect predictor of structural
degradation in AI-generated repositories, and neither detailed prompting nor high functional
correctness appears to interrupt the trend. A second finding from the same study, which they
term a \emph{Reasoning-Complexity Trade-off}, is counterintuitive: as models become more
capable, the code they generate becomes more bloated and more coupled, not less. Their conclusion
is that progress on this axis requires agents with explicit architectural oversight, which is
precisely the property no functional benchmark rewards.

\subsection{Architectural Metrics}
\label{sec:architectural-metrics}

The Chidamber--Kemerer (CK) metrics suite \citep{chidamber1994metrics} provides the standard
quantitative vocabulary for architectural quality: Weighted Methods per Class (WMC), Depth of
Inheritance Tree (DIT), Number of Children (NOC), Coupling Between Objects (CBO), Response for a
Class (RFC), and Lack of Cohesion in Methods (LCOM). For package-level analysis, Martin's
instability and abstractness metrics \citep{martin2003agile} supplement CK. Of these, CBO and WMC
are the primary indicators of architectural degradation, high CBO signals tight inter-class
coupling; high WMC signals bloated classes, and both appear with elevated frequency in
AI-generated code, as reviewed further in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-review}.

The RealBench benchmark \citep{li2026realbench} operationalises architectural quality at the
repository level through an Architecture@$k$ metric on a 0--4 scale. The best current agents
average around 2.25/4 (roughly 56\% of ideal), and performance drops sharply as repository size
grows: pass@1 falls from above 40\% for repositories under 500 lines to below 15\% for those
exceeding 2,000 lines. That non-linear scaling failure is one of the more concrete datapoints in
the literature about where architectural reasoning actually breaks down.

\section{Evolution and Current Capabilities of Coding Agents}
\label{sec:evolution}

The agent landscape has shifted considerably over a fairly short period (Figure~\ref{fig:timeline},
p.~\pageref{fig:timeline}). The difference between first- and second-generation tools is not just
incremental capability improvement, it is a change in the kind of task the tool is designed to
handle, which has direct implications for how the tools should be evaluated.

\subsection{First-Generation Assistants (2021--2022)}

GitHub Copilot, released in 2021 on top of OpenAI's Codex model, established the template: inline
suggestion of the next line or function based on the surrounding context. Amazon CodeWhisperer
(2022) added enterprise-oriented features, security scanning, licence attribution, while
maintaining the same reactive model. At this stage, neither tool exercised autonomous initiative;
they amplified what the developer was already doing. Benchmarks like HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021),
which measures pass@$k$ on 164 isolated Python problems, were a reasonable match for this
capability level.

\subsection{Agentic Platforms (2023--2025)}

The shift to agentic tools began in earnest with Devin (Cognition Labs, 2024), which demonstrated
end-to-end task completion, including shell execution, web browsing, and iterative debugging,
without per-step human guidance. Cursor integrated deep repository indexing with agentic editing.
Claude Code (Anthropic, 2025) brought a terminal-native agent with structured reasoning traces and
multi-file context management. OpenCode (2025) pursued an open-source composable architecture.
OpenAI's Codex CLI (2025) brought similar capabilities into a local agentic loop with shell access.

These tools differ from their predecessors in a way that matters for evaluation: they maintain
state across a session, decompose specifications into sequences of actions, invoke external tools,
and revise on failure. Applying HumanEval to them misses the dimension where they both succeed and
fail.

Peer-reviewed comparisons of these scaffolds against each other barely exist yet, which is part of
the gap this thesis addresses. What is available is engineering experience from the teams building
them. Anthropic's write-up on context engineering \citep{anthropic2025context} argues that an agent
operates under a finite attention budget, so which tokens reach the model is itself a design
decision, and names bloated or ambiguous tool sets as one of the most common failure modes in
deployed agents. This is a vendor report about a vendor's own product, and Anthropic builds one of
the three agents measured here, so it carries no evidential weight in this thesis; it is cited only
for the design claim it makes, which the empirical chapters test rather than assume. That claim has
a direct implication for evaluation: the agentic scaffolding is not a neutral wrapper around a
generative model, it is an independent variable.

\subsection{Functional Benchmark Performance}

On existing functional benchmarks, second-generation agents perform well, but how well depends
sharply on how the task is posed. The multi-agent AgentCoder framework \citep{huang2023agentcoder}
reaches 96.3\% pass@1 on HumanEval with GPT-4, using a programmer--test-designer--test-executor
loop. That result needs care: HumanEval operates at the function level, the scale at which
architectural deficiencies are least visible.

Widen the task even slightly and the numbers fall. BigCodeBench \citep{zhuo2024bigcodebench} asks
models to compose calls across 139 libraries under more complex instructions; across sixty
evaluated models the best score reaches roughly 60\%, against 97\% for humans, and the authors
conclude that LLMs cannot yet follow complex instructions to use function calls precisely.
LiveCodeBench \citep{jain2024livecodebench} attacks the problem from the contamination side,
scoring models only on problems published after their training cut-off, and uses that holdout to
expose overfitting to existing benchmarks, evidence that leaderboard standings are partly an
artefact of the evaluation set rather than a property of the model.

These results point the same way: the further a benchmark moves from the isolated function, the
less the headline numbers hold. SWE-EVO \citep{sweevo2025} shows how much performance changes when
the task shifts to long-horizon evolution, a leading agent that resolves roughly 73\% of
SWE-bench Verified issues resolves only about 25\% of SWE-EVO's multi-file evolution tasks (48
tasks averaging 21 changed files each).

\section{Evaluation of Code Quality and Architecture in AI-Generated Code}
\label{sec:evaluation-review}

There is now a growing body of work that goes beyond functional correctness to probe structural and
architectural properties of agent outputs. The following sections organise this work by evaluation
dimension. This thesis draws on all of it for motivation, but narrows its own empirical scope to
one part of the picture, \textbf{adherence to the five SOLID principles}, measured with
validated deterministic proxies, which the review below shows to be both the most
design-relevant and the least reliably measured dimension at repository scale.

\subsection{Principle Adherence and Architectural Quality}

Two recent studies bear directly on SOLID adherence in agent-authored code. \citet{pehlivan2025solid}
prompt several LLMs to detect SOLID violations across 240 manually-validated examples and find that
structurally-obvious principles (SRP, ISP) are detected reliably, that Dependency Inversion is the
hardest, and that accuracy falls sharply as code complexity rises. ``Beyond Functional Correctness''
\citep{beyond2026designissues} examines large projects generated by an AI IDE (Cursor),
averaging roughly seventeen thousand lines across a hundred-odd files, and reports that
functionally-working projects still carry design issues, including SRP-related large-method and
complexity smells, that would accrue maintenance cost over time. The common thread is that passing
tests and being well-structured come apart, which is the gap this thesis measures.

\citet{konrad2026vibe} frame the mechanism behind this. They coin \emph{vibe architecting} for
architecture shaped implicitly by how a prompt is worded rather than by deliberate design, and
identify the structural decisions, framework choice, scaffolding, integration wiring, that
agents make without anyone reviewing them as architecture. Their reading is that agents tend to
reproduce dominant patterns from training data rather than apply design principles with intent,
which predicts exactly the kind of pre-existing, unauthored structural debt this thesis observes at
the pull-request level (Chapter~\ref{ch:results}).

Repository-level benchmarks add the scale dimension. RealBench \citep{li2026realbench} evaluates
repo-level generation against real requirements and UML designs across 61 repositories, and reports
that architectural quality remains well below ideal and degrades as repository size grows, the
same non-linear scaling failure NITR observes for maintainability. None of these studies isolates
individual agents at pull-request granularity on a common corpus with validated proxies, which is
the specific gap addressed here.

\subsection{Code Smells}

The most useful recent framing of AI-generated smells is qualitative rather than a single density
ratio. \citet{zhu2026smells} argue that AI does not eliminate flaws so much as introduce a distinct
``machine signature'' of defects, recurring patterns including broken abstractions (creating an
interface, then bypassing it through concrete type references), systematic duplication,
over-engineering, and verbosity. The claim is not that these are simply worse than human smells but
that they arise from pattern mimicry, so they are more systematic and less visible to standard
review heuristics. The broken-abstraction pattern in particular is close to what this thesis
measures under DIP and OCP.

This suggests a boundary that the present study takes seriously in its design. Some of what agents
leave behind is \emph{structural}, expressible as a property of the code's shape, and therefore
in principle scannable. The rest is a matter of intent: code that works, and whose shape is
unremarkable, but which was never written to any coherent design idea. No static rule reaches the
second kind. That is the reason this thesis reports precision and recall for every proxy against an
independent reading judge, instead of treating a structural flag as a verdict about design.

\subsection{Repository-Scale and Human-Acceptance Evaluations}

ProjDevBench \citep{lu2026projdevbench} evaluates six coding agents on 20 end-to-end
project-development problems across eight categories, combining online-judge testing with
LLM-assisted review to score system architecture, functional correctness, and iterative
refinement. The overall acceptance rate is 27.38\%: agents cope with basic functionality and data
structures but struggle with complex system design, complexity optimisation, and resource
management. Its 20-problem corpus is too small for reliable comparison between individual agents,
which is one reason the present study works over hundreds of real merged pull requests instead.

Two studies work on the same AIDev corpus this thesis draws from, and together they mark the
boundary of what a mined agentic corpus has so far been used to establish.
\citet{mazloomzadeh2026agentic} compare agentic and human pull requests longitudinally, tracking
how merge rates diverge, which task categories agents are assigned, and how the characteristics of
their contributions shift over successive quarters. The study is a characterisation: it establishes
what agentic pull requests look like and whether they are accepted, using merge outcome and surface
properties of the request rather than any measure of the resulting design.

\citet{cynthia2026postmerge} take the next step and measure quality directly, analysing 1{,}210
merged agent-generated bug-fix pull requests with SonarQube, differencing the base and merged
commits so that only issues the pull request itself introduced are counted. Three of their findings
bear on the present design. Apparent differences between agents in raw issue counts largely
disappear once normalised by code churn, so an agent that writes larger changes will look worse
unless size is controlled. Code smells dominate over bugs, particularly at critical and major
severity, which places the problem in the design register rather than the correctness register.
And, most directly, \textbf{merge success does not reliably reflect post-merge code quality}. That
last result is an empirical warning against treating acceptance as a quality signal, and it is
tested independently here on a different quality construct and a corpus that includes the requests
maintainers declined (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

SWE-bench \citep{jimenez2024swebench} and its Verified variant remain the standard for agentic
evaluation on real-world software tasks, measuring what fraction of GitHub issues an agent can
resolve with all associated tests passing. The benchmark is rigorous and practically grounded, but
it evaluates issue-level repair rather than system-level construction. An agent that performs well
on SWE-bench may excel at targeted fixes while being poor at designing coherent architectures from
a specification, the tasks are different. This thesis treats SWE-bench scores as context, not a
sufficient proxy.

SWE-EVO \citep{sweevo2025} addresses long-horizon, multi-step maintenance and reports a large drop
from SWE-bench Verified ($\sim$73\%) to its evolution tasks ($\sim$25\%). SlopCodeBench
\citep{slopcodebench2026} tracks structural degradation across iterations directly, over 20
problems and 93 checkpoints across 11 models. Verbosity and structural erosion accumulate
monotonically as agents extend their own prior solutions, and agent code runs about 2.2$\times$
more verbose than comparable open-source projects. No model solved any problem end-to-end, and the
highest checkpoint solve rate was 17.2\%. Prompt-based mitigations did not stop the decay.

\citet{sawada2026maintenance} study what happens to agent-generated code after it is merged,
analysing over 1,000 files and roughly 3,200 changes across 100 popular repositories, drawn,
like this thesis's corpus, from the AIDev dataset. Three findings are relevant here. AI-generated
files are updated \emph{less} frequently than human-authored ones, and the updates touch only a
small fraction of file size. The modifications differ in kind: AI-authored files mostly receive
feature extensions, whereas human-authored code mostly receives bug fixes. And human developers
perform the large majority of the maintenance on both. The picture is therefore not one of agent
code visibly failing in production, but of it being extended by people other than its author,
which is exactly the situation in which structural quality, rather than test-passing, determines
cost.

METR's analysis of SWE-bench Verified \citep{metr2026sweverified} gives the most direct evidence
that benchmark scores overstate production readiness: roughly half of agent solutions that pass the
automated grader would not be merged by the repositories' own maintainers, about a 24-point gap,
and roughly twice the rejection rate of the human reference solutions. Because the agents were not
allowed to iterate on review feedback, this is a gap in evaluation framing rather than a fixed
capability ceiling; either way, it is part of why this thesis validates its structural proxies
against a reading judge rather than trusting a passing test.

\subsection{Prompting and Mitigation Studies}

How far prompting can move design quality is still open. Evidence from the detection side
\citep{pehlivan2025solid} shows prompt strategy has a large effect, an ensemble strategy helps
OCP, example-based prompts help DIP, so the framing of a request clearly matters; but that says
nothing about whether generation-time prompting holds up across a long task.

SlopCodeBench \citep{slopcodebench2026} is discouraging on this point: quality-aware prompts
improve initial quality but do not change the rate of structural degradation over successive
checkpoints. Prompting addresses surface compliance without reaching the underlying
architectural-reasoning problem.

Agentic linting loops, the agent writes code, runs a checker, and revises before committing,
are widely recommended in practitioner accounts, but no controlled study located for this review
measures their effect on design quality, as opposed to on lint-rule compliance. The gap matters for
this thesis in a specific way: a loop of that kind can only enforce what a checker can express, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} shows that three of the five SOLID principles are not reliably expressible
as a deterministic rule. An intervention that makes compliance the path of least resistance is
plausible on general grounds, but its reach is bounded by the quality of the instrument in the
loop, which is the thing this thesis measures.

\section{Research Gaps and Thesis Contributions}
\label{sec:gaps-contributions}

Three structural gaps run through the literature reviewed above, and they collectively define what
this thesis contributes.

\paragraph{Scale with real, attributable units.} Existing structural evaluations are either
functionally rigorous on small isolated tasks (HumanEval, SWE-bench) or structurally indicative on
small corpora (ProjDevBench's 20 problems). This thesis works over \textbf{4{,}245 real Java pull
requests across 1{,}010 repositories} from three agents, covering requests that were merged,
rejected and still open alike, so that each measurement is attributable to a specific agent
action rather than a synthetic task, and so that the effect of human review is measurable rather
than assumed.

\paragraph{Depth and measured construct validity, rather than shallow breadth.} Most studies
evaluate one or two quality dimensions and \emph{assert} that their metrics stand in for a design
principle; a few attempt many dimensions shallowly. This thesis instead goes deep on the single
most design-relevant and least-reliably-measured dimension, \textbf{SOLID adherence, all five
principles}, and treats the proxy-to-principle gap as something to \emph{measure}
rather than assume: every proxy is validated against an independent reading judge, reporting
per-principle precision and recall. The contribution is therefore not a wider dashboard but an
honest map of \textbf{where structural measurement of SOLID works} (crisp detectors for ISP and
LSP) \textbf{and where it only screens} (SRP, OCP, DIP), and of what does and does not raise
that precision, and which restrictions of a structural signal raise it.

\paragraph{Agent-level specificity.} The literature consistently conflates model capability with
agent capability, varying the underlying model while treating the scaffolding as a constant. In
practice, the scaffolding is not constant: Claude Code, Copilot, Cursor, Devin, OpenCode and
Codex implement materially different context-management strategies, tool-use patterns, and refinement
workflows. This thesis isolates those differences at the level of the agent, reporting per-agent
adherence controlled for repository mix so that a single dominant repository cannot masquerade as
an agent property. That the harness matters at all is no longer an assumption.
\citet{bensghaier2026harness} hold the language model constant and vary only the harness across 35
sequential releases, tracing quality fluctuations to particular development patterns and
architectural components and linking individual harness pull requests to measured shifts in
performance. What remains unsettled is whether that effect reaches \emph{design} quality, and no
published study measures it per principle on a shared corpus. Treating it as an open empirical
question, rather than assuming either answer, is part of what this thesis contributes:
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports where the three agents separate and where they do not.
