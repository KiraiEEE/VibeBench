\section{Summary of the Work}
\label{sec:ch5-summary}

This thesis set out to answer a question that functional benchmarks are not built to answer: does
the code that autonomous coding agents write for real projects hold up against the structural
design standards, the SOLID principles, that software engineering practice has developed over
five decades? Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction} motivated the question from the gap between benchmark
scores and production acceptance. Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} showed that the existing literature
either measures structural quality shallowly across many dimensions, or deeply on corpora too small
to separate agents. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} built a measurement design around that gap: one
deterministic proxy per principle, validated against an independent reading judge rather than
assumed to be faithful. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} realised that design over \textbf{4{,}245
analysable Java pull requests across 1{,}010 repositories}, written by three agents, and covering
merged, rejected and still-open requests alike.

Two design decisions distinguish the study from a conventional mined-corpus analysis. The corpus is
not restricted to merged code, so the effect of human review is observable rather than baked into
the sample. And construct validity is measured rather than argued: 1{,}400 flagged and unflagged
entities were read and labelled, with the reason for each verdict recorded, so that every rate in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} arrives with a known error profile.

The remainder of this chapter answers the three research questions (Section~\ref{sec:rq-answers}),
states what the thesis contributes (Section~\ref{sec:contributions}), gives a practical reading for
anyone choosing between agents or building on one (Section~\ref{sec:practical}), states the
limitations that bound every claim (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}), and closes with the directions
the work leaves open (Section~\ref{sec:future-work}).

\section{Answers to the Research Questions}
\label{sec:rq-answers}

\paragraph{RQ1, Do agents differ, where, and does review select on it?}
The agents differ, but the difference is narrower than a pooled comparison suggests. Claude Code
carries the highest pooled flag rate on all five principles and OpenAI Codex the lowest on four,
including no refused-bequest violation in 770 pull requests (Table~\ref{tab:agent-rates}).

That ordering is partly a difference in how much the agents change at once. Mean change size ranges
from 46.4 Java files for Claude Code to 10.9 for Codex, and flag rates rise steeply with size for
every agent, exactly the confound \citet{cynthia2026postmerge} identify when they normalise by
churn. Splitting the comparison by change size (Table~\ref{tab:size-control}) shows the ordering
holding in the largest band, where Claude Code flags 31.3\% of its Open/Closed changes against
8.9\% for Codex, and failing in the smallest, where Copilot leads on two principles and Codex on
Dependency Inversion. The defensible claim is therefore conditional: \textbf{on large changes the
agents separate in the order given; on small changes they do not separate at all.}

Three further qualifications bound even that. The agents worked in different periods, with median
pull request dates seven months apart, so agent and era cannot be separated here. The pooled and
mix-controlled figures disagree for Codex on two principles, and the mix-controlled figure is the
one to read. And Codex contributes a third as many analysable pull requests as Copilot.

The outcome axis returns a cleaner answer, provided the question is asked at the right
resolution. Once reconstruction method is held constant, merged pull requests are structurally
indistinguishable from those closed \emph{without review}, which are 1{,}006 of the 1{,}177
not-merged requests (Table~\ref{tab:outcome-stratum}). The two strata that do carry a maintainer's
judgement are small, 36 and 135 requests, and both run \emph{higher} than merged on Interface
Segregation and Dependency Inversion, so whether review itself attends to structure is a question
this corpus raises without answering. What it does establish is the methodological point: for these
signals a merged-only
corpus is not a biased sample of agent output, which could not have been established without
measuring the requests that were turned down.

\paragraph{RQ2, Can SOLID be measured at scale, and how faithfully?}
It can, with an operating characteristic that must be stated rather than collapsed into one number,
and the two halves of that characteristic point in different directions. Precision divides sharply:
Dependency Inversion reaches 0.890, and the other four sit between 0.330 and 0.420 as originally
composed. Recall, once each stratum is weighted back to its size in the corpus, divides just as
sharply: 0.733 to 1.000 for SRP, OCP and DIP, whose flag rates can therefore be read as upper
bounds, but 0.352 for Liskov and 0.552 for Interface Segregation, whose cannot
(Table~\ref{tab:validation}).

Two of the four can be repaired, and the labels themselves say how. Restricting the Liskov flag to
refused bequest, a base method that does real work, replaced by an override that only throws, gives
precision 1.000 on 64 flags across two independent samples. Requiring an interface to carry at
least 30 members raises Interface Segregation to 0.786. Both are restrictions rather than new
signals, and both were derived from reading false positives rather than from tuning against the
score.

Because both were fitted to a sample, both were re-scored on a holdout of 200 further entities per
principle, drawn with a different seed and excluding every previously judged row. The Liskov
restriction held exactly, 33 of 33; the width condition fell from 0.862 to 0.704, outside its own
holdout interval. A categorical rule survives being fitted and a numeric threshold does not, which
is the more general lesson of the exercise and the reason the pooled figures are the ones quoted
here.

The gap that remains is the substantive answer. A structural proxy asks a syntactic question and
the principles are semantic, and Open/Closed shows why that gap is not always closable: precision
is flat across every dispatch width, because whether a set of variants can grow is not a property
of how many branches it currently has.

A second axis answers the objection that a judge reading the same source can be wrong in the same
direction as the rubric. Three published analysers, run at their own defaults over 111{,}029 of the
same production types, place the class-level instrument \emph{inside} the disagreement they already
show among themselves: our rubric agrees with PMD at $\kappa = 0.574$ and with DesigniteJava at
$\kappa = 0.531$, while those two agree with each other at $\kappa = 0.384$
(Table~\ref{tab:tri-tool}). Two thirds of our flags are corroborated by at least one tool that
knows nothing about this study. Where the tools diverge, the cause is the unit rather than the
verdict: DesigniteJava charges the supertype for a substitutability fault where we charge the
subclass, and works at package granularity for cycles where we work at compilation-unit
granularity.

\paragraph{RQ3, Where does risk concentrate?}
Inside single units. Of roughly 4{,}100 single-responsibility violations and 270 substitutability
violations present in the Java code these agents touched, the agents introduced a violation in two
to four per cent of their pull requests (Table~\ref{tab:present-introduced}). The three
relation-level principles show it most sharply: 681 refused-bequest relationships exist in the
touched code, and pull requests introduced 16.

The explanation is structural. Single Responsibility and Open/Closed are properties of one unit,
which a single pull request can author whole. Liskov, Interface Segregation and Dependency
Inversion are properties of a relation between units, a base and its override, an interface and
its implementers, a cycle among modules, and authoring both ends of such a relation in one
contribution is rare. The consequence for practice is that per-change review can plausibly catch
intra-unit damage and structurally cannot catch inter-unit damage, because the evidence for the
latter is not present in any single diff.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}, leftmargin=1.6cm]
  \item A \textbf{validated, repository-scale measurement instrument for all five SOLID
  principles}, with every proxy's precision and recall reported against an independent reading
  judge rather than assumed. No prior study measures SOLID conformance, as opposed to detecting
  it in isolated examples, or measuring smells adjacent to it, on code that autonomous agents
  actually authored for real projects.
  \item A \textbf{corpus built on all three pull request outcomes}, which makes the review filter
  measurable instead of invisible, and the finding that for these signals the filter does not act.
  This is the direct answer to the survivorship-bias objection that a merged-only design cannot
  address from within.
  \item An \textbf{explicit map of where structural measurement of SOLID works and where it only
  screens}, including two detector refinements derived from labelled false positives and a
  principled account of why the same kind of refinement is unavailable for Open/Closed. The map is
  falsifiable and re-testable, which a single pooled accuracy figure would not be.
  \item An \textbf{empirical account of where agent-authored design risk concentrates}: inside
  single units, not across them, measured in a language chosen specifically to remove the standing
  objection that SOLID is an awkward fit for languages which do not mandate object orientation.
  \item A \textbf{result about language semantics and measurability}: three of the five signals
  used to detect Liskov violations in a dynamically typed language cannot occur in Java code that
  compiles. What a type system enforces determines which violations a program can express, which is
  a methodological result independent of SOLID.
  \item An \textbf{auditable validation procedure}. Every label is required to trace back to the
  source the judge was shown, every label carries its recorded reason, and a mechanical audit
  enforces both. This makes the most subjective step in the study checkable by someone who does not
  trust the person who performed it.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Practical Implications}
\label{sec:practical}

For a team choosing between coding agents, the SOLID profile in this corpus is a second axis
alongside functional pass-rate benchmarks rather than a replacement for one. The differences
between agents are real but small, and the more useful observation is the one that holds across all
three: agent-authored classes are flagged at roughly half the rate of the pre-existing classes
those agents edit. A reader who assumes agent output is the source of the structural problems in
these repositories has the direction wrong.

For a team building an automated review gate, the results are directly actionable in one respect
and directly cautionary in another. The single check worth adding today is \textbf{refused
bequest}: a subclass overriding a working inherited method with a body that only throws. It is
cheap to compute, it validated at 1.000 precision on 31 flags here and 0.823 on 62 flags in an
earlier round, and it corresponds to something a reviewer would object to if they saw it. Interface
width above roughly 30 members is a second usable signal. Conversely, a Single Responsibility or
Open/Closed flag from a detector built this way should open a conversation rather than block a
merge, since two thirds of such flags did not survive reading.

The broader implication follows from RQ1 and RQ3 together. Review does not appear to select on
these properties, and the violations that matter most accumulate across changes rather than
arriving in one. An automated structural check is therefore most valuable where human review is
structurally unable to look: across the history of a component, not inside a single diff.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The following bound every claim made above.

\paragraph{SOLID adherence is not the same thing as design quality.} This thesis measures
conformity to five principles articulated by one author and widely taught since; it does not
measure whether conforming code is cheaper to change, less fault-prone or easier to understand. No
step in the pipeline observes an outcome. The link from ``violates SRP'' to ``costs something
later'' runs entirely through the cited literature, and that literature is not unanimous:
Section~\ref{sec:code-smells} notes that God Class is markedly less fault-prone once class size is
controlled, which is exactly the confound a size-based rubric is most exposed to. A reader who
rejects SOLID as a quality model should read every result here as a measurement of \emph{stylistic
conformity} rather than of quality, and every comparison in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} remains valid
under that reading, because the same instrument is applied to every agent. What would settle the
question is a study that joins these flags to later defect or churn outcomes in the same
repositories, which the corpus makes possible and this thesis does not attempt.

\paragraph{The labels are model-produced, not human ground truth.} Every precision and recall
figure is agreement between a deterministic rubric and a large language model reading the source.
The judge can be wrong, and can be wrong in the same direction as the rubric. Two things constrain
this. A second, independent reading of 174 overlapping entities agreed with the first 89.7\% of the
time, which places a ceiling on how precisely any agreement figure can be known. And the
class-level instrument is compared against three independently developed analysers, which cannot
share the study's biases by construction; it agrees with each of them more closely than they agree
with each other (Section~\ref{sec:crosscheck-results}). That defence covers the
single-responsibility instrument, which is the one the external tools also measure. Open/Closed and
Interface Segregation have no counterpart in any published source-only Java analyser, so for those
two the reading judge is the only validation available. The figures should be read as
rubric-versus-judge agreement throughout, never as accuracy against ground truth.

\paragraph{Change size is a partial confound on the agent comparison.} The agents write changes of
very different sizes, and flag rates rise with size. Section~\ref{sec:agent-differentiation}
reports the comparison within size bands for this reason, and the pooled per-agent table should not
be read on its own.

\paragraph{Agent and era are confounded.} The median pull request date differs by seven months
across the three agents. An agent measured on older code faces different repositories, framework
versions and tasks. The per-agent ordering should be read with that in mind, and no claim is made
that it would survive a period-matched sample.

\paragraph{``Rejected'' is not ``refused on quality''.} Most closed pull requests were never
reviewed, which in practice means superseded, stale or abandoned. Only the
\texttt{changes\_requested} and \texttt{reviewed\_then\_closed} strata carry a recorded judgement,
and they are small. The outcome contrast is reported by stratum for this reason, and the conclusion
drawn from it is the narrow one: review does not appear to select on SOLID adherence.

\paragraph{Reconstructability differs by outcome.} 62.1\% of merged requests are analysable against
56.8\% of rejected ones, and the reconstruction methods available differ between them. The outcome
comparison is therefore made only within a matched tier. Without that control it would partly be a
comparison of which pull requests could be reconstructed rather than of what they contain.

\paragraph{Symbol resolution is incomplete, and unevenly so.} Median supertype resolution is 87.0\%,
but 718 pull requests resolve below 50\%, concentrated in framework-heavy projects. Liskov and
Interface Segregation under-count there. The direction is safe, unresolved supertypes cause
missed violations rather than invented ones, so the affected rates are lower bounds.

\paragraph{A single snapshot per pull request.} The design compares two trees. Slow architectural
erosion across many changes is invisible to it, which is precisely the process the intra-unit
finding suggests matters most for the relation-level principles.

\paragraph{One language, and one dataset.} The results are Java, drawn from AIDev. The framework is
language-parameterised and a second language is an additional fact extractor rather than a second
study, but nothing here demonstrates that the per-agent ordering transfers. A benchmark defined
over specific pull requests also degrades as the ecosystem moves: 18 requests were already
unrecoverable because repositories had been deleted or their history rewritten.

\paragraph{Small absolute counts on the relation-level principles.} Sixteen pull requests introduce
a Liskov violation and 195 a dependency cycle. These are reported as counts beside every rate so
that a reader can weigh them directly rather than trust a percentage a handful of pull requests
could move.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A churn-based signal for Open/Closed.} The finding that openness is uncorrelated
  with dispatch width removes the obvious threshold and suggests the replacement: a class edited
  across many pull requests is, by definition, not closed for modification. The corpus already
  carries the change history required to compute it.
  \item \textbf{A closed-set suppressor that recognises external specifications.} The existing
  suppressor recognises language builtins and fired on none of the flagged rows. The closed sets
  that actually occur are JDBC type codes, protobuf scalars, Unicode blocks and generated parser
  tables. Recognising specification-derived enumerations is a concrete and testable improvement.
  \item \textbf{Recall for the two blind detectors.} The weighted recall of the Liskov and
  Interface Segregation proxies rests on the few violations found in an unflagged stratum that is
  fifty times larger than the flagged one, which makes their intervals wide. A sample drawn only
  from unflagged entities, sized for that question alone, would narrow them; the three misses
  already found say where to look, at silent refusals rather than declared ones.
  \item \textbf{A second language with the same agent coverage}, to test whether the per-agent
  ordering is a property of the agents or of how they write Java. The pipeline shape ports; the
  obstacle is corpus availability rather than method.
  \item \textbf{A repository-scale unit of analysis.} The intra-unit finding implies that
  inter-unit damage accumulates outside the reach of any per-change measurement. Measuring a
  component across its history, rather than a diff, is the natural next design.
\end{itemize}

\section{Closing Remark}
\label{sec:ch5-closing}

The central result of this thesis is a distinction rather than a verdict. The design faults that
autonomous coding agents leave in real code are concentrated inside single units, a class doing
too much, a conditional that should have been an extension point, and are largely absent from
the relations between units that one change rarely gets to author in full. Most of what the
instruments find in these repositories was written by people, before the agent arrived.

That distinction held across three agents, across merged and rejected work alike, and in the
language most likely to break it. What remains open is whether it holds as the unit of measurement
grows: a repository maintained over months, by an agent and by the humans who edit its output
afterwards, is the scale at which architecture is actually won or lost, and it is not the scale at
which a pull request can be read.
